Why water temperature matters more than time

A two-degree difference can turn a sweet green tea bitter. Here's how to get it right at home, with or without a fancy kettle.

By Sana Okafor · October 2026 · 5 minute read

Most people think a bitter cup of tea means it was steeped too long. Sometimes it was. But far more often, at the tea room, we trace bitterness back to the water: it was simply too hot.

Tea leaves hold two kinds of flavor. The sweet, savory amino acids dissolve easily, even in cool water. The bitter, drying tannins need heat to come out. Pour boiling water on a delicate green tea and you pull out everything at once, bitterness first.

A rough guide

TeaWaterWithout a thermometer
Matcha and Japanese greens160–175 °FBoil, then wait 5 minutes
White tea175 °FBoil, then wait 4 minutes
Oolong195–205 °FBoil, then wait 1 minute
Black tea and pu-erh205–212 °FJust off the boil
Cool the water, not the tea. Once the bitterness is in the cup, waiting won't take it out.

The no-thermometer trick

If your kettle doesn't have a temperature setting, boil the water and pour it into an empty cup first. Each pour from one vessel to another drops it by about 10 degrees. Two pours and you're in green tea territory.

Then, and only then, think about time. Start with the suggestion on the tin, taste early, and adjust next time. You'll be surprised how forgiving tea is once the water is right.
